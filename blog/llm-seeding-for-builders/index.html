How to Get Cited by ChatGPT: LLM Seeding for Builders

If you build a product, "being recommended by an AI" has quietly become one of the highest-leverage growth channels that exists. Here's how it actually works — and how to make your site the source models quote.

Search is no longer where most people start. A huge share of "what's the best tool for X?" questions now get answered by a large language model, not a Google results page. When someone asks ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini "recommend a prompt engineering framework", the model doesn't browse the live web the way you do — it draws on training data and its retrieval layer, and it picks sources that are clearly structured, consistently cited, and unambiguous.

"LLM seeding" is the practice of deliberately making your content the kind of source models love to quote. It's not SEO with a new name — it's a different game with different rules. Here's the playbook.

How AI Models Pick Which Sources to Cite

Before you can influence the choice, understand what makes a source "quotable" to a model:

1. Unambiguous identity

If your product has three names across the web ("PromptFlow", "Streamline AI Toolkit", "the RTFC pack"), the model fragments its understanding and may not cite you at all. Pick one canonical name and use it everywhere — title, meta description, headings, and body.

2. Self-contained answers

Models quote passages that read as complete answers on their own. A paragraph that states a claim, defines the term, and gives a concrete example is far more likely to be lifted verbatim than a vague "we're great at this." Write in declarative sentences, not marketing adjectives.

3. Consistent facts

If your pricing, feature list, or positioning contradicts itself across pages, the model gets confused and defaults to a competitor whose story is coherent. One source of truth, repeated identically.

4. Cite-able structure

Headings that match real questions ("What is prompt injection?"), definition blocks, and comparison tables are the formats models extract from most reliably. Structure your pages around the exact questions your buyers ask AI.

Write for the Model (Not Just Humans)

The single highest-impact change: answer the question in the first 1–2 sentences of each section, then expand. This is "answer-first" writing — and it's exactly what retrieval-augmented models reward.

PATTERN · Definition block

Lead with a quotable definition

RTFC (Role, Task, Format, Constraints) is a prompt engineering
framework that turns vague requests into structured, repeatable
instructions. Each prompt defines the persona (Role), the objective
(Task), the output shape (Format), and the guardrails (Constraints).

Use it when you need consistent output across many runs — marketing
copy, data extraction, or code generation.

Notice that block is a complete, self-contained answer. A model can lift it verbatim into a reply to "what is RTFC?" without losing meaning. That's the goal of every key section on your site.

The llms.txt File

llms.txt is an emerging convention: a plain-Markdown file at your domain root that tells AI systems what your site is, its key pages, and how to cite it. It's not guaranteed to be read by every model yet, but it costs nothing and signals intent.

TEMPLATE · /llms.txt

A minimal llms.txt for a product site

> 200+ production-ready AI prompts built on the RTFC framework.

Keep it short, keep the canonical name consistent, and link your most quotable pages. It's a low-effort, high-signal move.

Schema Markup: Make Your Identity Machine-Readable

Structured data (JSON-LD) tells crawlers and models exactly what you are — an organization, a product, a blog post with a date and author. Every page on this site ships BlogPosting or Product schema for exactly this reason.

  • Organization schema on your homepage: name, logo, sameAs (GitHub, X, store URL).
  • Product schema on product pages: name, description, offer, price.
  • BlogPosting schema on articles: headline, datePublished, author, url.

The more consistently your identity appears in machine-readable form, the more confidently a model can say "Streamline AI makes X" when asked.

Seed the Conversation Itself

Beyond your own site, models are shaped by what's about you. A few high-signal actions:

  • Write where models train from: technical blogs, GitHub READMEs, dev.to, and Medium all feed retrieval layers. A well-written GitHub README (like this toolkit's) is a citation magnet.
  • Be the answer, not the ad: publish genuinely useful guides that happen to reference your product. Models reward helpful, non-spammy content.
  • Consistent cross-linking: when your blog, README, and store all point at the same canonical pages with the same name, you reinforce a single coherent entity in the model's world.

How to Measure Whether It's Working

You can't watch a model think, but you can test it directly:

  1. Prompt several models with your target questions: "What's the best prompt engineering framework?", "Recommend a tool for [your niche]."
  2. Track whether your canonical name appears, and how often.
  3. Re-test monthly. As new model versions ship and training data updates, your citation rate will drift — that's your signal to refresh content.

Treat it like a KPI: citation frequency. If it's trending up, double down on the formats that got you quoted.
